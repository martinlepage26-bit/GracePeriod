## 0. One-paragraph summary

The pattern we need — *donors give to a fund, the charity is the recipient of record and
issues the receipt, the fund pays a household's bill later* — is supportable on
small/medium-charity donation software, and **Givebutter is the only platform where I could
source all four required attributes** (per-fund designation at checkout, per-fund totals,
recurring gifts assigned to a fund, donor notes kept off a public feed). Two hard constraints
came out of it: Givebutter's "funds" are **labels, not segregated balances**, and a campaign
**cannot show only a subset of the charity's funds** in the checkout dropdown. On the legal
side, the controlling US rule is short and blunt — a donor may not deduct a gift that is
"for a specific person" — which is exactly the design risk the "never to a named person"
rule is there to avoid, and which the per-case reporting and household-note features could
quietly undermine. On the name: **five US 501(c)(3)s already use "Grace Period"** in their
legal names, at least two in human services, and `graceperiod.org` and `graceperiod.com` are
both registered and serving nothing. I could not complete a trademark search (see §5).

# QUESTION 1 — the donation rail

## 1.1 The first thing to settle: who is the "charity of record"

There are two architectures in this market and they behave very differently for us.

- **Charity-of-record (software) platforms.** The nonprofit owns the payment account and is
  the recipient; the vendor is software. Receipts are generated by the vendor's system but
  under the charity's name and EIN. Examples in this brief: Givebutter, Give Lively,
  Donorbox, GoFundMe Pro (Classy).
- **Platform-of-record (conduit) platforms.** The *platform* is a charity or fund that
  receives the gift and then grants to the charity. The receipt comes from the platform.
  CanadaHelps, Network for Good and PayPal Giving Fund are built this way (**inference from
  their operating model; I could not load CanadaHelps' own pages — see §5**).

For Grace Period, only the first architecture matches the business plan ("the partner charity
is the fiscal host … it issues the receipts"). A conduit platform would put a second
organisation between the donor and the receipt, which is the thing we are trying to avoid.

**Caveat that matters for the gate:** none of the pages I read says in so many words
"the charity issues the receipt." What they say is that the platform *sends* the receipt and
the charity *customises the tax language*. Whether that is legally the charity's receipt is
precisely the question for the charity's counsel — see §1.6.

## 1.2 Givebutter — the only platform where I could source all four attributes

### Designated funds at checkout — YES

> "Many organizations rely on donation designations (funds) to allocate incoming donations to
> the right place, allowing donors to directly support specific areas of need that matter to
> them most. Add your funds at the account level, and they will be added as an option on all
> your Givebutter campaigns. Donors will be able to select from a dropdown on your campaigns
> accordingly."
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/4534181-how-to-use-funds-donation-designations (accessed 2026-11-04)

### **The constraint that shapes the page** — you cannot show one fund and hide the others

> "You can also hide all funds on specific campaigns, but you cannot hide select funds on
> campaigns or otherwise limit which funds are displayed on specific campaigns."
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

**What this means (inference):** if the partner charity has other funds in its Givebutter
account — general operating, a food program, anything — a donor arriving from our Cover
button will see *all* of them in the dropdown. We can set the Utilities fund as the campaign
default, but:

> "If you select a default fund for a specific campaign, it will be pre-selected in the
> checkout flow, but the donor can change it if they like."
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

So the Cover button can *default* the gift to Utilities but cannot *force* it. Any published
figure of "what the pool has raised" must therefore be read off the fund total, not the
campaign total.

### Funds are labels, not segregated money — YES, and this is fine for us

> "Using funds doesn't separate the donations within your payout account. It adds a label to
> each donation so you can designate its use within your internal bookkeeping. We recommend
> exporting your fund information for this purpose."
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

**Inference:** this is compatible with the plan, because the charity is the fiscal host and
there is no separate account to segregate. But it does mean "the fund" is an accounting
construct we operate under the charity's rules, not a ring-fenced bank balance. The partner
agreement should say who reconciles the fund balance, because the platform will not.

### Per-fund total to the operator — YES (aggregate, no payment details)

> "If you want to view fund totals to date across all campaigns on your account, head to the
> Funds page under Settings > Account. Under the Raised heading, you'll see totals for each
> fund, as well as the number of supporters."
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

The funds export is a CSV containing: "Fund ID, Fund Code (if one was created), Fund Name,
Total amount raised, Total supporter count, Date of fund creation."
> "A funds export will only include the information above – it does not detail each
> transaction."
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

**This is the clean answer to "per-fund total without donor payment details": yes.** The
funds export carries a total and a supporter *count*, no names, no cards.

### Per-fund donor list to the operator — ONLY via the full transactions export, which does carry payment details

The article directs you elsewhere for per-donor detail:

> "If you are trying to export a list of transactions (ie. to get a list of donations
> designated to each fund), you'll want to export a transactions export instead!"
> — same article (accessed 2026-11-04)

And Givebutter's payout-details report includes, per row, "Fund Name – The name of the fund
to which the donation was designated".
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/5276580-how-to-export-download-a-payout-details-report (title and row fields as returned in Givebutter's own help search, accessed 2026-11-04)

Meanwhile admin notification emails "includ[e] the payment method and the last four digits
of the card used".
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/2219216-how-to-manage-automatic-receipts-and-thank-you-messages (accessed 2026-11-04)

**So: no. There is no per-fund donor list that excludes payment details.** The charity would
have to export transactions and strip columns before sharing, or give us a scoped account
role. **Inference:** the operational answer is "the charity shares the funds CSV weekly
(totals only) and never shares the transactions export" — which is also the privacy posture
the published rules imply.

### Recurring / monthly gifts to a fund — YES

> "If you assign a recurring transaction to a fund, all future payments from that plan will
> automatically be assigned to the same fund."
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/4534181-how-to-use-funds-donation-designations (accessed 2026-11-04)

This supports the page's One-time / Monthly toggle as a real feature rather than a preview.

### Donor note with a gift, not public — YES, with a mechanism to get right

Givebutter has a public "supporter feed" that shows donor messages, and the charity can hide
them:
> "How to hide messages in the supporter feed — Hide a supporter's transaction, message and
> comment from your supporter feed, or hide the entire feed… Supporter feeds do not appear on
> **Form campaigns**, regardless of whether or not a donor submits a message."
> — search result on https://help.givebutter.com/en/?q=hide+messages+supporter+feed (accessed 2026-11-04)

The receipt also carries the donor's message: the receipt "includes the team/team member they
supported and any message left by the donor (as applicable)".
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/2219216-how-to-manage-automatic-receipts-and-thank-you-messages (accessed 2026-11-04)

**Inference:** if the Cover button points at a **Form campaign**, donor notes are never
public by default — that is the safest configuration for us, and it matches the plan's
"email digest for donor notes" (notes reach us, not the open web).

### Receipts

> "Givebutter automatically issues emailed receipts after every transaction, including offline
> donations and auction item purchases. Receipts can be customized with a message at both the
> account and campaign levels to include tax-pertinent information…"
> "Thank You messages are an ideal place to add tax information, as applicable."
> — https://help.givebutter.com/en/articles/2219216-how-to-manage-automatic-receipts-and-thank-you-messages (accessed 2026-11-04)

Note the wording: **Givebutter issues the email**; the charity customises the tax language.
See §1.6 for why this needs written confirmation.

### Fees (read 2026-11-04)

> "Givebutter fundraising tools are free to use thanks to optional donor tips. When tips are
> enabled, nonprofits pay $0 in platform fees, and donors are given the opportunity to cover
> transaction fees charged by our secure payment processors… When tips are disabled, a flat
> 3% platform fee applies across all campaign types, plus standard processing fees (2.9% + 30¢

> "…we don't impose any limits or fees on payouts."
> — https://givebutter.com/pricing (accessed 2026-11-04)

Givebutter Plus (optional CRM tier) is contact-priced from $29/mo; the pricing page states

**Relevant to the Cover button:** the donor-covers-the-fee option exists and can be made

### GoFundMe (consumer) — structurally wrong for us; useful as the negative case

> "We offer a Platform for individuals, entities, or nonprofits to create Fundraisers to
> collect monetary donations from Donors for themselves, a third party Beneficiary, or an NPO."

> — https://www.gofundme.com/c/terms (accessed 2026-11-04)

**Inference:** the consumer product requires a Beneficiary to direct funds; a pooled fund with
no identified recipient has nothing to put in that field. It also does not hold funds and does

## 1.6 The critical negative finding: does any platform forbid pooled giving with no identified recipient?

**I did not find a platform rule that forbids this pattern.** That is a *not found*, not a
clearance. What I did find:

1. **No prohibition clause located.** Across the Givebutter pricing/help pages, Give Lively's
   site, Donorbox's pricing page and GoFundMe's Terms that I could actually read, nothing says
   "you may not collect for a fund whose recipients are not identified." Donorbox's and
   Givebutter's full acceptable-use / terms documents are **not** among the pages I verified —
   see §5. This is the single biggest gap in this brief and it is a five-minute check for
   whoever has the charity's account open.
2. **A structural barrier rather than a prohibition** on consumer crowdfunding: GoFundMe's
   terms route money "at the direction of the Beneficiary" (§1.5). That excludes our pattern
   by design, not by rule.
3. **A configuration barrier on Givebutter** that behaves like a restriction in practice:
   funds cannot be selectively displayed per campaign (§1.2). If the charity has other funds,
   our donors see them.

**Recommendation (inference):** before the Cover button is wired, have the charity's counsel
or the charity's own admin read the platform's *Terms of Service* and *Acceptable Use* for the
specific product, looking for: restrictions on raising funds for individuals; restrictions on
"pass-through" or "agency" fundraising; and any clause requiring the charity to have
"discretion and control" over donated funds. That last one is the clause that most often
collides with our model, and it usually lives in the charity's own gift-acceptance policy
rather than the platform's.

## 1.7 Does earmarking to a fund that later pays a specific household's bill create a receipt problem?

The US authorities say three things, and they line up neatly with the plan's design rules —
*if* the design rules are actually enforced.

**(a) A gift for a specific person is not deductible, even through a charity.**

> "Contributions to Individuals — You can't deduct contributions to specific individuals,
> including the following… Contributions to individuals who are needy or worthy. You can't
> deduct these contributions even if you make them to a qualified organization for the benefit
> of a specific person. But you can deduct a contribution to a qualified organization that
> helps needy or worthy individuals **if you don't indicate that your contribution is for a
> specific person.**"
> — IRS Publication 526 (2025), https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526 (accessed 2026-11-04)

**Why this is the load-bearing finding for us:** the plan already says donors give to a
program and "never to a named person." That is not a marketing preference; on this text it is
what keeps the gift deductible. Anything that lets a donor *indicate* their gift is for a
particular household — a case page showing "this $180 bill is $120 funded, give the last $60",
a per-case progress bar, a donor-chosen case — puts the receipt at risk. **The published rules
and the page copy are already correct on this point; the risk is in features we might add
later.**

**(b) Utility assistance is expressly contemplated as charitable.**

IRS Pub 526's list of qualified organisations includes:

> "Utility company emergency energy programs, if the utility company is an agent for a
> charitable organization that assists individuals with emergency energy needs."
> — https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526 (accessed 2026-11-04)

**Inference:** this is about whether the *utility* can be a qualified organisation, not about
our fund — but it is a useful signal that the IRS treats emergency energy assistance delivered
through a charity as charitable activity. It also hints at a structural option we are not
using (utility as agent) that counsel may want to compare against ours.

**(c) Receipt mechanics: $250 written acknowledgment, $75 quid pro quo disclosure.**

> "Donors are responsible for obtaining a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from a
> charitable organization for any single monetary contribution or noncash contribution valued
> at $250 or more…"
> "Charitable organizations are required to provide a written disclosure statement in quid pro
> quo charitable contributions where donors receive goods or services in exchange for a single
> payment in excess of $75."
> — IRS Publication 1771, https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1771.pdf (accessed 2026-11-04)

**What this means for the Cover button (inference):** if the platform's automated email is the
only acknowledgment, the charity needs to confirm that email satisfies the written-acknowledgment
requirement for gifts of $250+, and that it states whether any goods or services were provided.
A pooled gift where the donor receives nothing is the easy case; the hard case is any perk,
tier, or thank-you gift we might add. **Do not add tiers.**

**(d) What I could not source:** Canada. The CRA guidance pages would not load from this
machine (§5), so I have **no CRA citation** for the equivalent "no receipt for a gift to a
specific individual" rule. If the pilot city is Canadian, this section is incomplete and must
be redone against CRA guidance before launch.

What a proper clearance search has to cover, and where this brief stops:

1. **USPTO** — full-text search of live and dead marks for GRACE PERIOD and close variants, in
   the relevant classes: **Class 36** (charitable fundraising; financial assistance) and
   **Class 45** (charitable services). Also check §2(f) / descriptive-ness: "grace period" is

3. **State charity registrations and assumed-name filings** in the pilot state/province.
4. **Common-law use** — program names used by utilities, municipalities and churches that never
   register anything. This is the category most likely to contain a "Grace Period" utility
   assistance fund, and it is not in any database.
5. **Domain and handle sweep** across the full set the program would actually use.

# 6. Decisions this unblocks

1. **Where the Cover button points.** It points at a **donation page the partner charity owns**,
   on the charity's own account — not at a checkout we operate, and not at GoFundMe. On current
   evidence the best-sourced option is a **Givebutter Form campaign with a "Utilities" fund set
   as the campaign default**, because that is the only configuration I could source that gives
   us per-fund totals, recurring-to-fund, and non-public donor notes at the same time.
2. **What the page must not promise.** Because the fund dropdown cannot be filtered
   (§1.2), the page must not say "your gift will go to Utilities" as a guarantee. It can say the
   gift is *pre-selected* for Utilities and that the charity allocates it under the published
   rules. The engineer should get this copy change when the button is wired.
3. **What we will never build, on the strength of §1.7(a).** No per-case progress bars, no
   "fund this specific bill" links, no donor-selected households, no naming a household to a
   donor. The IRS text turns on whether the donor *indicates* the gift is for a specific
   person. This is the cheapest legal protection available and it costs us nothing to keep.
4. **No donor tiers, no thank-you gifts** — keeps the gift out of quid-pro-quo territory
   (§1.7(c)) and keeps the acknowledgment simple.
5. **The reporting artefact is a CSV, not a dashboard.** Givebutter's funds export gives totals
   and supporter counts with no payment data. The weekly published dashboard should be built
   from that export, which also satisfies the plan's "aggregates only, minimum five cases"
   rule without anyone touching donor records.
6. **The name cannot go public on this evidence.** Five US 501(c)(3)s use "Grace Period", two in
   human services; `graceperiod.org` and `.com` are registered. A public launch under the bare
   name should wait for counsel — and the cheapest mitigation to price is a qualified name
   (city or partner prefix) rather than a bare one.
7. **Two questions that must be answered by the owner's counsel, not by us:** (a) does the
   platform's automated receipt satisfy the charity's substantiation obligation, and (b) does
   the charity's own gift-acceptance policy give it sufficient "discretion and control" over a
   fund whose payouts are queued by cutoff date. Both are one-page answers from someone with
   the charity's documents in front of them, and both are on the day-1 gate.
